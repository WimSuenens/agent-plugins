# Design brief

Apply the principles below to a new, unrelated project. Do not reuse any
specific colours, fonts, or copy from a reference design — invent your own
palette, typography and words from these rules.

## Concept

Atmosphere over object: a near-black surface that never reads as neutral
grey, lit from behind by a handful of enormous, faint colour blooms. The
page's personality should come from a deliberate tension between two
scales of the same shape language — the big structural containers read as
strict and square, while the smaller chrome living inside them (controls,
inputs, product surfaces) is generously rounded. Neither reads as the
"real" style on its own; the contrast between them is the identity.

## Colour strategy

Base the ground on a dark neutral with one deliberate colour cast (a
tint you'd notice if it were removed, not a literal black), lit by blooms
that are large, faint and soft-edged — if you can point to where a bloom
ends, make it bigger and fainter. Carry every interactive affordance on a
single accent ramp (a hue moving toward a neighbouring hue), used nowhere
else. Separately, allow yourself a wider decorative palette purely to tell
apart peer items in a set (cards, categories, chart series) — but that
palette must never touch a button, link or heading, or the one accent ramp
stops meaning "you can act on this."

## Type strategy

Run nearly the entire page — from the smallest label to the largest
headline — on one workhorse sans, controlling hierarchy with size, weight
and two distinct tracking values (tight on headings, wide on eyebrows/
labels) rather than switching faces. Introduce a second, more expressive
face for exactly one word, one time, on the page — a single accented word
in your most prominent headline. Use it nowhere else; a signature used
twice stops being a signature. Deliberately let some genuinely small type
(labels inside a dense product mock, say) sit below comfortable reading
size — the point of that content is to be recognised as "a real, detailed
interface," not to be read, and enlarging it for legibility destroys that
read.

## Shape & elevation

Let radius be the loudest single lever you have: keep the outer shells —
section containers, big cards — square or barely rounded, and let the
smaller controls and product chrome nested inside them be noticeably
softer. Skip hard borders in favour of translucent neutral (a white or
black at low opacity) or a thin gradient line for separating regions —
nothing on the page should have a flat, fully-opaque rule around it.

## Motion

Every element enters once — fade and rise together, staggered a beat apart
sibling to sibling — and then never moves again on scroll. Reserve looping,
ambient motion for one or two small, self-contained elements (a marquee, a
pulsing status dot, a slow colour cycle inside a chart) rather than the
page at large. Suppress everything, including anything animated via SVG
rather than CSS, when the viewer has asked for reduced motion.

## Voice

Keep section labels to one to three words, uppercase, wide-tracked — never
a full phrase. Build big headlines as two clauses split across two lines,
so a colour or weight shift can land on the payoff word. Name problems as
short noun phrases and resolve them with a verb, so the "before" reads as a
symptom and the "after" reads as an action. Give quantities specific,
slightly odd values rather than round ones — a round number reads as
invented. Stack reassurances or guarantees in groups of three, each under
five words.

## Apply it

Take a brief for a completely different product or audience and design it
from these rules: your own tinted-dark ground and bloom lighting, your own
square-shell/round-chrome tension, your own one-word type signature, your
own accent-ramp-only interactivity rule, and your own fire-once entrance
choreography.
