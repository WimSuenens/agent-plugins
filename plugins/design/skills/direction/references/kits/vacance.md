# Design brief

Apply the principles below to a new, unrelated project. Do not reuse any
specific colours, fonts, or copy from a reference design — invent your own
palette, typography and words from these rules.

## Concept

The product is an absence, not an object — the whole page argues for
something the user *doesn't* get more of, not something they get more of.
Structure the page as a small number of full-bleed chapters that alternate
between exactly two grounds, and cut hard between them — never blend or
gradient from one to the other. The handoff itself should feel like the
argument: two states, no in-between.

## Colour strategy

Choose exactly one accent hue and give it every job in the interface:
display type, small text, buttons, anything that needs emphasis on any
ground. Don't let it specialise into "the button colour" or "the link
colour" — it should be recognisable as *the* accent everywhere it appears.
Then deliberately withhold a second hue that shows up elsewhere in the
content (photography, illustration, data) from ever entering the UI layer —
if the content already owns a colour, the interface doesn't get to borrow
it. That exclusion is what keeps the one accent legible as a decision
rather than a default.

## Type strategy

Carry the whole page on a single sans-serif family, and get your size/
weight hierarchy from a width or optical axis rather than stacking on a
second display face — heaviness and presence should come from how wide or
condensed a cut is, not from a jump to a different font. Reserve a second,
monospaced family for exactly one kind of content: values a computation
produced (a count, a coordinate, a timestamp, a live reading) — and never
let it set a sentence a human wrote. That reservation should be absolute;
the moment the mono face sets prose, it stops reading as "instrument
output."

## Shape & elevation

Stay almost radius-free — let hairlines and hard clip edges do the framing
instead of rounded corners, so the one place you do add roundness (pills on
actionable controls, say) actually stands out. Build your one real
elevation effect as a *recess*, not a raised card: layer several inset
shadows so the object reads as a hole cut into the surface, something you
look into rather than something sitting on top. Don't mix a recessed device
with an outward drop shadow anywhere near it — a raised shadow beside a
recessed one contradicts the "cut into" reading and collapses the effect.

## Motion

Pick one easing curve and one duration and use them for every entrance on
the page, no exceptions. Size each entrance's travel distance to the actual
content it's revealing (clear the line-height of the type it moves under)
rather than defaulting to a round, stock distance. Keep ambient/looping
motion to one or two genuinely small, self-contained devices (a countdown,
a progress rail) rather than porting a large scroll-driven engine — a
static fallback that matches those small loops beats a half-built version
of something more cinematic.

## Voice

Label sections with a number plus a short, specific claim — never a topic
word. Give each headline exactly one word that carries the whole idea, and
mark that word visually every time (same treatment, same hue) so a reader
learns to scan for it. State any computed value in exactly one place on the
page — if a caption names a feeling and an instrument names a count, don't
let a second caption restate the same number in words; two independent
statements of one fact can drift out of sync as soon as either becomes
dynamic. Write coordinates, ranges and timestamps so they read like
instrument output, not prose — terse, exact, unexplained.

## Apply it

Take a brief for a completely different product or audience and design it
from these rules: your own two grounds, your own one accent and its
exclusion, your own width-axis type system, your own recess-only elevation,
your own single easing pair, and your own numbered-chapter voice.
