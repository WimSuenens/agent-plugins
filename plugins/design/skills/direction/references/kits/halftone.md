# Design brief

Apply the principles below to a new, unrelated project. Do not reuse any
specific colours, fonts, or copy from a reference design — invent your own
palette, typography and words from these rules.

## Concept

The product should be the ornament: whatever technique or craft the page
is selling, the page's own type and imagery should visibly perform that
technique, rather than merely describing or decorating with it. Nothing on
the page is decoration for its own sake — every visual device demonstrates
the thing being sold. Treat the layout as a set of physical sheets stacked
on top of one another rather than a flat scroll: each section gets its own
faint ground colour, distinct enough to separate one "sheet" from its
neighbours but never bold enough to be nameable on sight.

## Colour strategy

Build a large family of near-identical faint ground tints — enough that
every section can sit on a different one — and treat "a tint you could
put a name to" as a sign you've gone too far; the restraint itself is what
sells "many sheets of the same stock" instead of "a slideshow." Reserve
exactly one true accent hue for anything interactive — controls, links,
eyebrows — and spend a single strongly saturated dark ground exactly once,
on the one section making an atmospheric or emotional case rather than a
practical one. Keep a separate, larger family of secondary hues whose only
job is distinguishing peer items in a set (a catalogue, a set of options)
— none of them may ever touch a control, or the one true accent stops
meaning "you can act on this."

## Type strategy

Pair one high-contrast, high-personality display face against one plain,
geometric workhorse face, and push the display face to an extreme end of
whatever weight or optical range it offers — the extreme is what gives it
a period-specific, crafted feel rather than a generic "nice font" feel.
Tune weights to odd, specific values rather than rounding to standard
steps; the imprecision reads as deliberate calibration, not carelessness.
Establish two or three tracking registers with clearly different values,
so each is legible as a distinct role at a glance rather than by
comparison.

## Shape & elevation

Require three ingredients at once for anything to read as "one physical
layer resting on another": a wide, soft, faint drop shadow; a crisp, thin
highlight inset along the top edge; and a hairline border. Treat all three
as mandatory together — a shadow alone reads as a generic UI card, not as
a sheet of material sitting on another sheet. Split your radius values by
the *role* of the surface (card, control, nav, chip, plate) rather than by
size, so a future variant can square one role while leaving another round.

## Motion

Use one easing curve throughout, with a small family of durations rather
than one flat number, and make staggered delays accelerate rather than
grow linearly, so a fourth or fifth element in a sequence doesn't lag
noticeably behind the first. Grant yourself exactly one slow, ambient
loop for the entire page, and spend it on a single, small recurring
"imperfection" — one element that visibly, repeatedly performs the flaw
your product is known for.

## Voice

Build every headline as two short lines with exactly one word per headline
carrying a visible "flaw" treatment (the demonstrated imperfection from
your concept) — never two words, and almost always the final word. Give
section labels a definite article plus a plain noun, two or three words,
never a full sentence. Run body copy in a fact-then-aside rhythm: a true,
useful first sentence, followed by a shorter second sentence that
playfully undercuts it without retracting it — repeat that shape across
every body paragraph on the page. State quantities precisely, and let one
of them be self-deprecating or absurd on purpose, sitting in the same row
as the straight statistics. Give any named tool, machine or process in
your copy a bit of character — a name, a mood, a history — rather than
describing it purely functionally. Devote real layout space to failures or
rejects, each labelled with the specific reason it didn't make the cut,
rather than only showing successes. Avoid abstract prestige vocabulary
entirely — reach for concrete, physical, trade-specific words instead.

## Apply it

Take a brief for a completely different product or audience and design it
from these rules: your own family of near-identical section grounds, your
own one-true-accent-plus-peer-distinguishing-hues split, your own extreme-
weight display face paired with a plain workhorse, your own three-
ingredient elevation, your own single ambient "flaw" loop, and your own
fact-then-aside voice.
